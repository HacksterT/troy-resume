Stage 1.4

Setting up Copilot

Leave signed in the right way, knowing what your version of Copilot can see, with one recurring task set up to reuse.

Quick review 15 minutes with the demo

  • Sign in with your university work account, not a personal Microsoft account. With the work account, enterprise data protection applies: your prompts and Copilot's responses are not used to train the underlying AI models, and they stay under the university's Microsoft 365 controls.
  • Check before you paste anything: a green shield at the top of the chat and a "Work" label under your profile mean you are in the protected work version.
  • Microsoft Copilot Chat (until recently called Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat) comes with many organizations' Microsoft 365 subscriptions. It works from the web plus what you give it: files you upload, or the email or meeting you have open.
  • The paid Microsoft 365 Copilot license goes further. It can draw on your mail, calendar, chats, meetings, and files without being handed them, and it adds the full Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and OneNote.
  • To see which you have, sign in at m365.cloud.microsoft and scroll to the bottom of the home page. The label under your name reads Copilot Chat (Basic), M365 Copilot (Basic), or M365 Copilot (Premium). Only Premium is the paid license.
  • For what CMU allows in Copilot and what data it may hold, check the "What CMU Permits" page in the team's Learn-Copilot channel.
  • Ways Copilot holds context beyond one chat: files you upload or reference; a notebook (Copilot Notebooks) that gathers documents, OneNote pages, and web links so Copilot answers from that set; a page (Copilot Pages) that turns a chat answer into an editable document you can share; saved prompts; and agents you build with standing instructions and sources.

Where people get stuck

  • Too many things called Copilot. The free consumer Copilot, Copilot Chat for work, and the paid Microsoft 365 Copilot can look almost the same on screen. What matters is which account you signed in with and which label you have.
  • Signed in with the wrong account. On a home computer or a personal browser, you may be in consumer Copilot with a personal account, which has its own privacy settings, including whether your conversations are used for training. No green shield, no university content.
  • "It can't find my file." Copilot Chat without the paid license cannot see your OneDrive, mail, or Teams on its own. Having Copilot inside Word is not the same thing either: the M365 Copilot (Basic) label gives you Copilot in the apps, but you still hand it the content. This is how it is built, not a malfunction.
  • Licensed Copilot finds whatever you can open. With the paid license, Copilot can surface any file you already have permission to view, including files someone shared too widely years ago. Copilot does not create the oversharing, but it makes it easy to find.
  • Files inside a shared agent. If you upload a file into an agent and share the agent, anyone you share it with can get at what is in that file.

  4. Set up one recurring task two ways: save the prompt so it can be reused, and create a notebook holding the files that task always needs.
  5. Set up the same task in ChatGPT and Claude side by side and compare how each platform holds context.

Discussion 30 minutes with your questions

  • Which account were you signed into when you first opened Copilot? How would you check on a home computer?
  • Which recurring task did you set up, and did a saved prompt, a notebook, or an agent fit it best?
  • If Copilot could search every file you have access to, what might it turn up that you would not want in a summary?

Pearls high yield

  • Green shield, work account, then paste.
  • Copilot Chat sees what you give it. Licensed Copilot sees what you can open.
  • If a file is overshared, Copilot can find it.
  • Check What CMU Permits before you rely on it.

Stage 1.5

Working patterns in Copilot

Leave with three tested prompts for real tasks, and a sense of where Copilot beats the other tools and where it does not.

Quick review

  • Copilot is strongest when the task depends on content that already lives in Microsoft 365: an email thread, a meeting, a document. The more of that content your license lets it reach, the more useful it is.
  • Email threads: open a long thread in Outlook and select Summarize (some versions say Summary by Copilot) at the top. The summary carries numbered citations that link back to the emails it drew from.
  • Meetings: Copilot in a Teams meeting can summarize the discussion, list who said what, and suggest action items. After the meeting ends, it can answer questions only if the meeting was transcribed.
  • Drafting: in Outlook, start a message and choose Draft with Copilot, adjust length or tone, then edit before you send. In Word, type / to pick one of your own files for Copilot to draft from.
  • Research scoping: Copilot Chat searches the web and cites its sources. Use it to map a topic, list the questions worth asking, and find search terms, then check the sources yourself. The paid license adds research agents such as Researcher.
  • Prompts that work: Microsoft's own guidance is a clear goal, plus context (why you need it, who it is for), source (which file, thread, or meeting to use), and expectations (length, format, tone).

Where people get stuck

  • Treating the summary as the thread. A summary reports what was said. It can miss a decision buried in an attachment, a change of tone, or what nobody said. Click a citation before you act on it.
  • The same prompt works in one place and not another. "Summarize my emails about the curriculum review" may work in Outlook and fail in Copilot Chat in the browser, because each place can reach different content depending on your license.
  • Not naming the source. "Find the budget document" leaves Copilot guessing, and it may pick an older or similar file. Name or reference the exact file, thread, or meeting.
  • Drafts that sound like nobody. Copilot's drafts lean generic. Give it an example of your own writing or a clear audience, and plan to edit.
  • Using it for everything. For work that does not depend on your Microsoft 365 content, such as long original writing or analysis, compare it with ChatGPT or Claude before settling on one tool.

Live demo

  1. Summarize a long sample email thread in Outlook, then click a citation to check one point.
  2. Draft a reply with Draft with Copilot, change the tone, and edit it before sending.
  3. In Word, draft a one-page summary from an existing document by referencing it with /.
  4. Scope a research question in Copilot Chat and open two of the cited sources.
  5. Run one of these tasks on ChatGPT and Claude side by side and compare the result and the checking time.

Discussion

  • Which of your three tasks did Copilot handle best? Was it the one that depended on your own mail, files, or meetings?
  • When a summary missed something, what was it, and how did you catch it?
  • After seeing the same task on all three platforms, which task would you give to ChatGPT or Claude instead of Copilot?

Pearls high yield

  • Copilot is at its best inside your own mail, files, and meetings.
  • A summary is a lead, not the record. Click the citation.
  • Name the source in the prompt.
  • No transcript, no meeting answers afterward.
