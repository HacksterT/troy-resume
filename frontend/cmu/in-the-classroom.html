Stage 2.4

Teaching students to use AI well

Leave with one assignment that has students use AI and then judge its output against a professional standard.

Quick review 15 minutes with the demo

  • Your students will supervise staff, vendors, and systems that use AI. Judging AI output is a professional skill for a health administrator, not only an academic integrity question.
  • Treat AI literacy as a learning outcome you can name and assess: use a tool for a defined task, check what it produces, and decide what to keep.
  • The strongest assignments have students use AI and then critique the output against a standard they already know: a rubric, a regulation, a course framework, an organization's policy, a data set.
  • Grade the critique and the decision. The AI output is raw material, not the work being graded.
  • Ask for a short disclosure: which tool, what they asked, what they changed, and why.

Where people get stuck

  • "If I require it, I am endorsing it." Requiring a critique of AI output teaches skepticism. Students learn where the tool fails by finding the failures themselves.
  • No standard to check against. "Evaluate the AI's answer" with no yardstick produces opinion. Name the standard: the rubric, the rule, the framework from this week, the organization's own policy.
  • Students who will not or cannot use it. Offer a second path: you supply the AI output and they critique it. The outcome you are assessing is the judgment, not the typing.
  • Critiques that are themselves AI-written. Ask for things that only real engagement produces: specific lines quoted from the output, a check against a named source, and a decision with a reason tied to the course.
  • Real data in the prompt. Working adults may paste information from their employer. Put the rule in the assignment: no patient, employee, or confidential employer information.

Live demo

  1. Ask an AI tool to draft a short operational recommendation from a health administration case, such as a staffing plan or a response to a patient complaint.
  2. Critique it live against a rubric: what is right, what is missing, what is wrong, and what would cause a problem if it went out as written.
  3. Turn that into a student assignment using one pattern: use AI for this task, check it against this standard, decide what to keep and justify it, disclose what you did.
  4. Show the second path: the same assignment with AI output you supply.

Discussion 30 minutes with your questions

  • Which assignment in your course already has a standard students could hold AI output against?
  • What would you accept as evidence that a student actually checked the output?
  • In the roles your students hold now or will hold, where will they be signing off on work that AI helped produce?

Pearls high yield

  • Grade the judgment, not the output.
  • No standard, no critique. Give them the yardstick.
  • Students who will not use AI can critique output you supply.
  • They will sign off on AI-assisted work as administrators. Teach it now.

Stage 2.5

AI in the live and online classroom

Leave with one short AI activity for a class session or discussion board, and a plan for students who cannot or will not use the tool.

Quick review

  • Keep activities short and structured: one task, a set time, and one question students answer afterward.
  • Demonstrate AI in front of students, including when it gets things wrong. Watching you catch an error teaches checking better than telling them to check.
  • Plan for uneven access. Some students pay for a tool, some use a free version, some use a university account, and some have none. Skill varies just as widely.
  • Decide ahead of time when AI is out, and say why: reasoning through a problem on their own, reflecting on their own experience, explaining their understanding aloud.
  • AI-written posts are common on discussion boards. Redesign the prompt rather than trying to police the posts.

Where people get stuck

  • A live demo that goes wrong. That is often the most useful part. Narrate the error and how you caught it. Keep a screenshot ready in case the tool is down.
  • Assuming everyone has the same tool. Paid, free, and university versions can behave differently. Design so the activity works in pairs, in groups, or on one shared screen run by you.
  • Policing the discussion board. AI detectors are not reliable enough to support an integrity decision. OpenAI withdrew its own detector in 2023 for low accuracy, and a Stanford study found detectors flagged human-written essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated more often than not. Change the prompt instead.
  • Generic prompts invite generic posts. "Discuss leadership in quality improvement" can be answered by any tool. "Describe one quality problem at your workplace, without identifying anyone, and say where this week's framework fits it and where it falls short" is much harder to hand off.
  • Putting it away without a reason. Students accept a limit more readily when you tell them what skill the task builds and why AI would get in the way.

Live demo

  1. Rewrite a generic discussion prompt so it requires the student's own experience, a specific course reading, and a reply to a classmate's specific point.
  2. Run "AI posts first": post an AI answer to the week's question and ask students to find what it gets wrong, leaves out, or would not work in their organization.
  3. Show an in-class activity: groups run the same prompt, compare outputs, and report one difference and one error.
  4. Run the same activity on a single shared screen, the way you would for a class with mixed access.

Discussion

  • Which of your discussion prompts could an AI tool answer well without the student? How would you change it?
  • What is one class moment where you would ask students to put AI away, and how would you explain it to them?
  • How would you include a student who has no access to the tool, or who does not want to use it?

Pearls high yield

  • Let students watch it fail, and watch you catch it.
  • Design for the student with the least access.
  • Fix the prompt, not the post.
  • When AI is out, say what skill the task builds.
