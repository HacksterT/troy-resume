Stage 1.4

Setting up ChatGPT

Leave knowing which account you are using, what its settings do with your data, and with one project set up for a recurring task.

Quick review 15 minutes with the demo

  • There are two ways in: a university-provided account, where CMU offers one, or a personal account you sign up for yourself. Before using either for work, check the "What CMU Permits" page in the team's Learn-ChatGPT channel.
  • On a personal account, the setting called "Improve the model for everyone" decides whether your new conversations can be used to train OpenAI's models. It applies to your whole account, on every device.
  • University and business accounts are managed by the institution. OpenAI says it does not train on Business, Enterprise, or Edu content by default. The institution's administrators set how long chats are kept, and may be able to access, export, or delete them.
  • Memory lets ChatGPT carry details from one chat into later chats. You can see what it remembers, delete items, or turn memory off in Settings.
  • Temporary Chat is a clean slate. It does not use or create memories, does not appear in your history, and is not used for training. OpenAI may keep a copy for up to 30 days for safety.
  • Custom instructions are standing preferences, such as your role and preferred tone, applied to all your chats.
  • File uploads give it documents to work from, in a single chat or in a project.
  • A project is a workspace for one ongoing effort. It groups related chats, files, and instructions, and remembers the chats and files inside it. Project instructions override your general custom instructions inside that project.
  • A custom GPT is a configured version of ChatGPT with its own instructions and reference files, built to be reused or shared. Creating new GPTs is not available on personal accounts. In university and business workspaces it depends on what the administrators allow.

Where people get stuck

  • Memory carries over between chats. Something you mentioned weeks ago can shape today's answer. Deleting a chat does not delete a memory saved from it. Use Temporary Chat when you want a clean start, and check what it remembers from time to time.
  • Which data controls apply to which account. The training setting is for personal accounts. On a university account, the university's agreement and administrators decide what happens to your data, and administrators may be able to see your chats. Check which account you are signed in to before you paste anything.
  • Projects versus custom GPTs. A project is your own workspace and keeps the history of its chats. A custom GPT is a reusable tool: it does not use your memory, custom instructions, or past chats, so every conversation starts fresh. For one person's recurring task, start with a project.
  • Project-only memory is decided once. A project can be set so it ignores your saved memories and chats outside it, but that choice is made when you create the project. A shared project switches to project-only memory and cannot switch back.
  • What it actually reads in a file. Charts, images, and scanned pages inside a PDF may not be read, depending on your account type and where you added the file. Files added to a project are read as text. Ask what it sees on a specific page before trusting a summary.

Live demo

  1. Open Settings and walk through memory, custom instructions, and data controls. Point out which ones a university account controls for you.
  2. Ask "What do you remember about me?" in a normal chat, then open a Temporary Chat and ask again.
  3. Create a project for one recurring task, such as weekly course announcements. Add standing instructions and one example file, then run a request in it.
  4. Upload a PDF that contains a chart and ask what the chart shows. Compare the answer with the page.
  5. Set up the same recurring task in Claude and Copilot and compare where each keeps standing instructions.

Discussion 30 minutes with your questions

  • Which account are you using for work, and what does the "What CMU Permits" page say it may hold?
  • Which recurring task did you pick for your project, and what belongs in its standing instructions?
  • Did anything ChatGPT remembered about you surprise you? Would you keep memory on for work?

Pearls high yield

  • Know which account you are signed in to before you paste anything.
  • Memory follows you between chats. Temporary Chat does not.
  • One recurring task, one project, with standing instructions.
  • Ask what it can see in a file before you trust the summary.

Stage 1.5

Working patterns in ChatGPT

Leave with three prompts that work in ChatGPT on tasks you do every week.

Quick review

  • Drafting. Give it the full brief from stage 1.2 and ask for two or three versions so you can choose.
  • Editing. Say what to change and what to keep. Canvas opens your draft in an editor beside the chat, where you can highlight one passage, ask for a change to just that part, and see what changed. Type "use canvas" in your request to open it.
  • Critique. Ask it to review as a specific reader, such as a skeptical committee member or a working adult student, and to list the weakest points first.
  • Summarizing documents. Upload the file, say who the summary is for and what decision it supports, and ask it to say where in the document each point comes from.
  • Research scoping. Use it to map a topic, list questions, and suggest search terms. When it searches the web it shows citations you can open.
  • Meeting prep. Give it the agenda and background papers and ask for a one-page brief, likely questions, or talking points.
  • It does well at fast first drafts, rewording for a new audience, outlines, and turning rough notes into a structured document.
  • Prompts that work name the reader, the task, the material, and the format, and end with "ask me any questions before you start."

Where people get stuck

  • Polished is not the same as right. Drafts read well even when a date, number, or policy detail is wrong. Check anything you will send or publish.
  • Summaries that miss the point. Without a stated reader and purpose, a summary comes back generic and can leave out the one item you needed.
  • It agrees with your draft. Asked "is this good?", it tends to say yes. Ask for the three weakest points, or the argument a critic would make.
  • Cited web answers still need checking. Search results can be incomplete, outdated, or wrong, and an answer can misstate its own source. Open the citation.
  • Saving the prompt without its context. A prompt that worked often depended on the file or background you gave it. Note what you attached when you save it.

Live demo

  1. Draft a short announcement from a full brief, then ask for a version for a different audience.
  2. Open the draft in canvas, highlight one paragraph, and ask for a change to just that part.
  3. Upload a committee report, ask for a summary that supports one decision with page references, and check two of the points.
  4. Ask for a critique of the draft from a skeptical reader.
  5. Run the same summary task on Claude and Copilot and compare the three results.

Discussion

  • Which of your three tasks gave the best result, and which needed the most fixing?
  • Where did ChatGPT struggle on your work, and did a better prompt fix it?
  • Which of your prompts could a colleague reuse without changes?

Pearls high yield

  • Ask for the weakest points, not whether it is good.
  • A summary needs a reader and a purpose.
  • A citation is a lead, not proof. Open it.
  • Save the prompt with a note on what you attached.
