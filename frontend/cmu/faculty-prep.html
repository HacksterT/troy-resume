Stage 2.1

AI policy for your course

Leave with a draft AI statement for one course that a student can follow without guessing.

Quick review 15 minutes with the demo

  • A syllabus AI statement tells students what AI use is allowed, under what conditions, and how to disclose it. Without one, each student guesses, and they guess differently.
  • Three common positions. Permitted: use is open, with disclosure. Conditional: use is allowed for named tasks, such as brainstorming or editing, and not for others. Prohibited: no AI use for this work.
  • Choose per assignment, not only per course. Start from what the assignment is meant to show the student can do.
  • Say what disclosure looks like: which tool, what it was used for, and what the student changed.
  • Start from CMU's public guidance: Planning for AI in Teaching and Learning.

Where people get stuck

  • A ban you cannot observe. If the work is done at home on a laptop, a prohibition mostly binds the honest students. Where the skill matters, design the assignment so AI is of little help (stage 2.3).
  • Vague verbs. "Use AI responsibly" tells a student nothing. Name the tasks: "You may use AI to check grammar. You may not use it to write your analysis."
  • Inconsistency across a program. A student in three courses may face three different rules in one term. Agree on shared terms and a default across the MHA or DHA program, and let a course depart from it on purpose and say why.
  • Leaving out the reason. Students follow a rule more readily when it says what the assignment is for.

Live demo

  1. Ask an AI tool to draft a conditional-use AI statement for a named MHA course.
  2. Rewrite its vague lines as named tasks a student can check against.
  3. Add a short table: assignment, AI position, what to disclose.
  4. Ask the tool to read the statement as a confused student and list the questions it would have.

Discussion 30 minutes with your questions

  • Which of your assignments would you put in each of the three positions, and why?
  • What should a student's disclosure look like, and what would you do with it once you have it?
  • What should be the same across the program, and what should be left to each instructor?

Pearls high yield

  • Silence is a policy. Students fill it in differently.
  • Decide by assignment: what is this meant to show?
  • Name tasks, not values.
  • Disclosure says which tool, what for, and what changed.

Stage 2.2

Designing with AI

Leave able to draft course materials faster with AI while the standard stays yours.

Quick review

  • AI is useful for first drafts of learning objectives, cases, discussion prompts, rubrics, and worked examples.
  • You stay the author and the judge. You decide what is accurate, what fits the course, and what is good enough to give students.
  • Brief it as you would a new teaching assistant: course, level, learners (often online working adults), the outcome, and limits such as length or setting.
  • Health administration cases need to be plausible to people who work in the field. Check numbers, regulations, and organizational details against sources you trust.

Where people get stuck

  • Generic cases. Without detail, AI writes a hospital that could be anywhere. Give it the setting, size, payer mix, and the decision you want students to face.
  • Invented facts. Statistics, rules, and named organizations in a draft case may be wrong or made up. Verify each one, or label the case as fictional.
  • Rubrics that do not separate work. Levels that say "good," "better," "best" do not help a grader. Each level should describe work you could tell apart on the page.
  • Objectives that do not match the task. AI will write an "evaluate" objective for an assignment that only asks students to recall. Check the verb against what students actually do.

Live demo

  1. Brief a tool for a short MHA case on a staffing decision at a rural hospital.
  2. Mark up the draft live: what is wrong, generic, or unverified.
  3. Ask for a four-level rubric tied to two course objectives, then tighten it.
  4. Ask for one weak and one strong sample answer, and test whether the rubric tells them apart.

Discussion

  • What did the draft get wrong that only someone who knows the field would catch?
  • How much of the time saved went back into revising?
  • Should students be told when course materials were drafted with AI?

Pearls high yield

  • AI drafts. You author and judge.
  • A specific brief gets a specific case.
  • Test a rubric with a weak and a strong sample answer.
  • Verify every fact in a case, or call it fiction.

Stage 2.3

Assessment in an AI world

Leave knowing which of your assessments AI can complete for a student, with one redesigned.

Quick review

  • Run your own assignment prompt through an AI tool. If it produces passing work in a few minutes, the assignment shows little about the student when done unsupervised.
  • AI does well on summaries, standard essays, generic reflections, and short factual answers.
  • AI helps less when the work depends on the student's own workplace, a live conversation, what was said in class, staged drafts with your feedback, or a decision the student must defend.
  • Four redesign options. Authentic: applied to the student's real organization. Process-based: drafts, notes, and revisions are part of the grade. Oral: a short live or recorded explanation. Applied: the student uses AI and critiques what it produced.
  • AI detection tools are not a sound basis for integrity decisions.

Where people get stuck

  • Trusting a detector score. OpenAI withdrew its own AI text classifier in 2023, citing its low rate of accuracy. A 2023 study that tested a range of detection tools concluded they are neither accurate nor reliable, and that disguising the text, such as by paraphrasing, makes them worse.
  • Who gets flagged. A 2023 study in the journal Patterns found that common detectors labeled more than half of essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated, while classifying essays by US eighth graders nearly correctly. Multilingual and international students carry the most risk.
  • Aiming for AI-proof. No assignment stays AI-proof as tools improve. Aim for AI use that is either built in and visible, or of little help.
  • Oral assessment sounds impossible online. A five-minute recorded explanation, or a short live check with a sample of students, can be enough.

Live demo

  1. Paste a real assignment prompt, with no student work, into an AI tool and show what it produces in a minute.
  2. Grade that output against the real rubric.
  3. Redesign live: add the student's own workplace, a staged draft, or a short oral defense.
  4. Run the redesigned prompt again and compare what the tool can now do.

Discussion

  • Which of your assignments did AI pass? Did that surprise you?
  • If you suspected AI use, what would you do without a detector?
  • What does each redesign option cost you and your students in time?

Pearls high yield

  • Run your assignment through AI before your students do.
  • A detector score is not evidence.
  • Build AI in, or make it of little help. AI-proof does not last.
  • Process and conversation are harder to fake than a finished product.

Stage 2.6

Feedback, scholarship, and the faculty role

Leave knowing where AI belongs in feedback and scholarship, and the lines it must not cross.

Quick review

  • Feedback: AI can help you build a comment bank, reword a comment so it is clearer or kinder, or write general feedback on errors common across a class. The judgment on each student's work stays yours.
  • Student data: do not paste identifiable student work, grades, or feedback into a tool CMU has not approved for that data. Confirm with CMU which tools are approved for what.
  • Authorship: ICMJE and COPE both state that AI tools cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot take responsibility for the work. Authors who use them disclose how, and remain responsible for everything submitted.
  • Journal policies differ by publisher and change over time. Read the policy of the journal you are submitting to, each time.
  • Peer review is confidential. NIH prohibits its peer reviewers from using generative AI to analyze or critique grant applications. Check a journal's reviewer policy before using AI on a manuscript you are reviewing.
  • IRB: if an AI tool will handle participant data, or AI is part of the study, describe it in your protocol and ask your IRB before you start.

Where people get stuck

  • "I removed the name." Federal guidance counts indirect details, such as a date of birth, as identifying when they can trace a student. In a small cohort, an employer and job title may be enough.
  • Letting AI grade. A tool that scores student work makes a judgment you are accountable for. Assess the work yourself, then use AI to help word the comments.
  • Citing the chatbot. ICMJE advises that AI-generated material should not be cited as a primary source. Find and cite the original.
  • Where to disclose. Put it where the journal asks, often the methods section or the cover letter: which tool, and what it was used for.

Live demo

  1. Write three common feedback points for an assignment, with no student work, and ask AI to turn them into a clear, supportive comment bank.
  2. Open the author guidelines of a health administration journal and find its AI policy.
  3. Ask AI to summarize that policy, then check the summary against the policy itself.

Discussion

  • Where is the line for you between AI helping with feedback and AI giving feedback?
  • What does a journal in your field require, and did anything surprise you?
  • What would you need to know from CMU before using AI with any student data?

Pearls high yield

  • If it could identify a student, it stays out of unapproved tools.
  • You grade. AI can help you word it.
  • AI cannot be an author. You answer for every word.
  • Read the journal's AI policy every time you submit.
