What is true of every AI platform: how these tools work, what context is, how to check what they give you, and how to choose a tool.

Stage 1.1

Ground rules and orientation

Leave knowing what these tools actually do, and what must never go into one.

Quick review 15 minutes with the demo

  • An AI chatbot writes its answer one piece at a time, choosing what is most likely to come next given everything in the conversation. It is not looking the answer up unless it says it is searching the web.
  • That is why it can be fluent and wrong at the same time. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy.
  • Ask the same question twice and you may get two different answers. That is normal, not a malfunction.
  • The platforms differ in where they get information, what they remember, and what happens to what you type.

Where people get stuck

  • "It's the same as a search engine." Search finds pages that exist. A chatbot writes new text, which may or may not match any source.
  • Free versus university accounts. A personal free account and a university-provided account can handle your data very differently. Use the tool CMU provides for anything work-related, and check what it allows.
  • What counts as student data. Pasting an email thread that names a student and their grade is student data, even if you only wanted help with the wording. Removing the name is not always enough when the details identify the person.

Live demo

  1. Ask two platforms the same work question side by side and compare.
  2. Ask one of them the same question again and show that the answer changes.
  3. Ask about something very recent and show the difference between a tool that searches the web and one that does not.

Discussion 30 minutes with your questions

  • What difference did you notice when you asked two platforms the same question? Which answer would you trust, and why?
  • Name one task in your job where you would never use AI. What makes it off limits?
  • Where in your week is there information you would have to strip out before you could use AI on it?

Pearls high yield

  • It predicts text. It does not look things up unless it says it is searching.
  • Fluent is not the same as correct.
  • Use the CMU-provided tool for work, and know what it allows.
  • If it could identify a student, it does not go in.

Share and next 15 minutes

Share: one difference you noticed between two platforms.

Stage 1.2

Context

Leave able to brief an AI tool the way you would brief a capable new assistant.

Quick review

  • The tool knows only what is in the conversation, plus anything it has saved in memory. It does not know your course, your students, your audience, or your standards unless you say so.
  • A good brief covers the goal, the audience, the background, the constraints, an example if you have one, and the format you want.
  • Files you attach are context too. So is everything earlier in the same chat.

Where people get stuck

  • Leaving out the audience. "Write an announcement" and "write an announcement for working adult graduate students who are new to AI" produce very different results.
  • Long chats drift. After a long conversation, earlier instructions get diluted. Start a new chat for a new task.
  • More words is not more context. A clear goal and one good example beat three paragraphs of explanation.
  • Not letting it ask. Ending a brief with "ask me any questions you need before you start" often surfaces what you forgot to say.

Live demo

  1. Run a one-line request, then the same request written as a full brief, and compare.
  2. Add "ask me questions before you start" and show what the tool asks.
  3. Continue a long chat on a new topic, then start fresh, and compare.

Discussion

  • Which part of the brief made the biggest difference to your result?
  • What context does an AI tool never have about your work that a colleague would?
  • When is it faster to just do the task yourself than to write the brief?

Pearls high yield

  • The tool knows only what you tell it.
  • Audience and format are the most often forgotten parts of a brief.
  • New task, new chat.
  • End the brief with: ask me any questions before you start.

Stage 1.3

Checking the output

Leave with a checking habit that matches how much an error would cost.

Quick review

  • AI tools can invent facts, numbers, quotations, and references that look entirely real.
  • Check in proportion to the stakes. A draft email needs a read. A claim you will repeat to students or put in a report needs its source.
  • Checking a reference means opening it and confirming it says what the tool claims, not just that it exists.

Where people get stuck

  • The reference exists but does not say that. This is more common than a fully invented reference, and harder to catch.
  • Asking another AI to check. A second tool tends to share the same blind spots. It can catch some errors, but it is not verification.
  • It agrees with you. These tools lean toward telling you what you seem to want. Ask it to argue the other side or find the weakness in your reasoning.
  • Checking costs time. If checking takes longer than doing the work yourself, AI was the wrong tool for that task.

Live demo

  1. Ask for a short summary of a topic with references, then check two of the references live.
  2. Push back on a correct answer ("are you sure?") and watch whether it caves.

Discussion

  • How many of your references held up? What kind of error did you find?
  • Where in your work is an AI error harmless, and where is it costly?
  • How would you teach a student to check AI output?

Pearls high yield

  • Check in proportion to the stakes.
  • Open the reference. Existing is not the same as supporting the claim.
  • A second AI is not a fact-check.
  • It tends to agree with you. Ask it to argue the other side.

Share and next

Try: ask for a short summary of a topic you know well, with references, and check every reference.

Share: the error you found, or the reference that surprised you.

Stage 1.6

Choosing a tool, and going beyond chat

Leave able to pick the right tool for a task, and to judge whether a new feature is worth your time.

Quick review

  • Choose by the job. A tool built into the software where your files and mail live is strongest when the task depends on those files. Other tools may be stronger for drafting, analysis, or longer documents.
  • Agents are AI tools that take actions, not just write text: searching, filling in, sending, scheduling. The more a tool can do on its own, the more its mistakes matter.
  • Judge a new feature by the time it saves after you have checked its work.

Where people get stuck

  • Looking for one best tool. There is not one. Most people end up using two for different jobs.
  • Counting only the time to generate. The real cost is writing the brief plus checking the result.
  • Letting a tool act without a check. Anything that sends, submits, or changes a record on your behalf needs a review step before it acts.

Live demo

  1. Run one recurring task on two tools and compare the result and the checking time.
  2. Show an AI feature built into everyday software and discuss whether it earns its place.

Discussion

  • Which task did you pick, which tool, and why?
  • What would have to be true for you to trust a result without redoing it?
  • What is one task you would hand to an agent, and one you never would?

Pearls high yield

  • Choose the tool by the job.
  • The real cost is the brief plus the checking.
  • Anything that acts on your behalf needs a review step first.
